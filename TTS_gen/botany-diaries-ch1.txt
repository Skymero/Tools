The room smelled wrong, but every sensor on the wall insisted Kellie was imagining it.

That possibility carried more weight than she wanted it to.

She had already caught herself entering the same moisture reading twice that evening. The error had been small enough to survive a quick glance—31.4 percent for tray seven, then 31.4 again for tray eight—but the trays shouldn't have matched. Seven had spent most of the afternoon beneath a hotter section of the grow array, while eight used a lighter substrate that dried faster. When Kellie pushed the handheld probe back into tray eight and watched the reading settle at 28.9, irritation tightened behind her eyes.

The plants were behaving normally.

She wasn't.

She corrected the log, then checked the specimen tags even though she knew she hadn't mixed them up. Seven. Eight. When she looked back at the tablet, the numbers took a fraction longer than usual to come into focus.

Enough.

The Conservatory seemed to agree. Above her, the artificial daylight had begun fading into night cycle as the building redirected power toward cooling pumps and thermal storage. The change was subtle—slightly dimmer grow lamps, a deeper mechanical vibration passing through the floor as another bank of pumps came online—but Kellie had spent enough nights in the nursery to recognize it.

She checked the time.

10:43 p.m.

She had arrived a little after seven that morning.

Kellie did the subtraction once, disliked the answer, then caught herself doing it again to make sure she hadn't added an hour somewhere.

That was when a shadow crossed her tablet.

“You're still here?”

Bram Vale stood opposite the propagation bench with a coffee in his hand.

Kellie stared at the cup before she bothered with his face. “You asked me to stay.”

“Right.”

“That was fourteen hours ago.”

Bram glanced toward the wall clock, not quite long enough to convince her he was surprised. He had spent most of the afternoon walking investors through the nursery while Kellie tried to keep a nutrient reservoir from drifting acidic. Each time he passed, he had been describing what *we* had accomplished with the drought cultivars.

Kellie had eaten noodles with one hand while recalibrating a dosing pump with the other.

Bram still had enough energy for coffee.

“Has it really been fourteen?”

She lowered the tablet. “Would you like the socially acceptable answer or the one involving this moisture probe?”

His attention dropped to the metal instrument in her hand.

“Socially acceptable.”

“Yes.”

He nodded, but didn't leave.

That was the first warning.

The second came when he shifted the coffee into his other hand.

“I need a favor.”

“No.”

“You don't know what I'm asking.”

“I have historical data.”

“Genetics sent over a trial group this afternoon. Twenty-four plants from the new drought-adaptation line. They need overnight monitoring.”

“Then Genetics should monitor them.”

“They're short-staffed.”

“So are we.”

“You're already—”

Bram stopped.

Kellie waited.

He reconsidered.

“—familiar with this type of work.”

“Excellent recovery.”

“Thank you.”

“It wasn't praise.”

“I'll survive.”

There were days when Kellie wished Bram were simply incompetent. Incompetence would have made leaving easy. She could have packed her books, deleted his number, opened her shop and never wondered whether she had abandoned an opportunity she wasn't ready to replace.

Instead, Bram actually knew what he was doing.

Three years earlier, Kellie had spent two days investigating what she thought was fungal damage across a propagation batch. Bram had picked up one leaf, rubbed the underside between his fingers, smelled it, and told her to check the manganese concentration.

She had run the analysis twice because she wanted him to be wrong.

He wasn't.

Worse, he had stayed after closing and shown her what he had noticed: the pattern of chlorosis, the location of the spotting, the way the damage followed new growth instead of old.

She still used that lesson.

That Bram had hired her.

The one standing across the bench now had apparently learned that every new skill Kellie acquired was one more task he no longer had to do himself.

He slid a transfer packet toward her. “Room Three.”

The room number pulled Kellie's attention away from her irritation.

“Room Three isn't cleared for unsupervised transfer trials.”

“You'll be supervising.”

“I mean by the people who engineered them.”

“They sent documentation.”

“Documentation doesn't become qualified personnel because you put it near me.”

Bram's expression shifted. Not much, but enough.

Kellie picked up the packet.

The plants had been engineered to modify stomatal behavior under drought conditions, reducing water loss while maintaining chemical communication between tissues. Useful work. Potentially very useful if the line could survive outside the protected agriculture zones, where reclamation teams were trying to force life back into soil that had spent decades accumulating salt, heat damage, and whatever the last war had left behind.

She skimmed farther.

Nothing in the transfer notes justified overnight observation.

Kellie went back to the first page.

Then the second.

Still nothing.

“If they're stable,” she said, “why am I watching them?”

Bram drank his coffee.

There it was.

She lowered the packet. “What happened?”

“Nothing happened.”

“Bram.”

“They had some irregular transpiration after transport.”

“What kind?”

“They don't know.”

“And Genetics sent them here anyway?”

“They don't think it's dangerous.”

Kellie waited.

Bram's mouth tightened.

“Fine. They *don't know* if it's dangerous.”

That was different.

More importantly, it was interesting.

Kellie hated that her brain reacted to both facts at once.

Transport shock could alter transpiration. So could mechanical root damage, humidity changes, light-cycle disruption, temperature stress or a dozen side effects from the modified stomatal pathway itself. If the behavior existed before transport, though, it should have appeared in the research notes. If it only began afterward, something had changed.

The questions began organizing themselves before she consciously agreed to investigate them.

Which was exactly how Bram kept winning.

Her notebook sat beside the tablet. When she reached for it, the pages slipped past the nursery logs and stopped on a floor plan she had drawn eight months earlier.

Four hundred and eighty square feet.

Propagation bench along the back wall. Low-light stock on the north side. Consultation table near the front window, but far enough from the glass that customers couldn't roast their purchases before reaching the door.

Her shop.

Not Bram's nursery. Not a research department. Hers.

The finances had worked when she ran them. Rent was unpleasant but manageable. The water allocation worked. Licensing worked. Even environmental control became reasonable if she delayed buying the second propagation rack.

She could have signed the lease.

Instead, she had decided another research season would make her better prepared.

That season had almost ended.

Now the experimental drought line sat in Room Three.

One more useful project.

One more thing to learn.

Then she'd be ready.

Probably.

“Kellie?”

She realized Bram was watching her.

She closed the notebook.

“Four hours.”

Relief reached his face before he could hide it.

“And I'm coming in late tomorrow.”

“Fine.”

“Put it in writing.”

His relief disappeared. “Seriously?”

“The last time you approved a comp morning, you called me at eight-thirty because somebody flooded propagation.”

“That was an emergency.”

“You had closed the drain valve.”

Bram pulled out his tablet.

Kellie waited until the approval appeared on hers before collecting the transfer documents and heading toward the sealed growing rooms.

Normally she would have stopped at bay six. Its moisture sensor was still drifting high whenever the misting system activated, probably because condensation was getting through a badly sealed connector that Engineering had classified as low priority eleven days earlier.

Tonight, she kept walking.

The sensor could be wrong tomorrow.

Room Three couldn't.

Kellie scanned her badge, and the door released with a soft pressure change. Warm air moved outward as the ventilation equalized, bringing the usual combination of damp substrate and fertilizer salts with it.

Something else caught high in her throat.

She stopped before crossing the threshold.

The sensation came before the smell itself: a dry chemical irritation behind her nose, similar to leaning over concentrated alcohol before realizing the container was open. It wasn't strong enough to make her cough. If she hadn't already been tired, she might not have noticed it.

That thought made her hesitate.

Kellie backed out and checked the environmental panel.

No volatile-organic warning. No particulate alarm. Oxygen and carbon dioxide both sat comfortably inside tolerance.

Safe, according to the equipment.

Except equipment could only detect what somebody had designed it to detect, and the plants inside Room Three had been engineered precisely because somebody wanted them to do something ordinary plants couldn't.

She pulled the transfer packet closer and entered.

Twenty-four specimens occupied ceramic containers beneath adjustable-spectrum lamps. Whoever had designed them had not wasted much effort making them attractive. Short stems kept their structures compact. Thick pale leaves reduced exposed surface area, and the root architecture described in the transfer notes was built to chase moisture deeper than most commercial plants could tolerate.

Kellie liked them immediately.

They looked like they had been built for work.

She started with specimen one.

The first several behaved closely enough to the published baseline that her attention began drifting toward the irritated spot in her throat. Transpiration was slightly reduced, as expected. Root moisture remained stable. Leaf temperatures varied, but not enough to matter.

Twelve ran warmer than eleven, though its substrate was also drier. That made sense: less available water meant less evaporative cooling through transpiration.

Fourteen went the other direction, running cooler despite similar root moisture. Kellie checked its stomatal conductance and found greater opening than the neighboring specimen.

Good.

Expected variation.

Then she reached seventeen.

The first temperature reading made her check the position of the lamp.

29.1 degrees.

Nearly two degrees warmer than sixteen.

Direct radiation could account for that much if seventeen was catching more light, but the canopy geometry didn't support it. She repositioned the meter and checked again.

29.0.

If the plant were water-stressed, the warmer leaf would make sense. Closing its stomata would reduce transpiration and trap more heat.

Except the substrate wasn't dry.

Kellie checked it again.

Plenty of water.

Maybe delivery was restricted farther down the line.

She checked irrigation pressure.

Normal.

Seventeen shared the same manifold as sixteen anyway. If the feed line were failing, both plants should have shown it.

Kellie leaned closer.

“So what are you doing?”

Under the inspection light, something reflected along the underside of the leaf.

At first she assumed condensation. The room was humid enough for it, and the temperature difference could create small deposits around the stomata. But condensation should have formed across the cooler surfaces first.

This leaf was warm.

She adjusted the light.

A droplet formed while she watched.

Tiny at first, almost level with the pore. It grew until surface tension pulled it into a bead, but instead of rolling down the leaf, the edge began thinning.

Kellie leaned closer.

The droplet flattened.

Then disappeared into a faint haze.

Her concentration sharpened.

Another formed several millimeters away.

Same process.

This wasn't water collecting from the room.

Something was coming *out* of the plant.

Kellie straightened too quickly and struck her thigh against the bench. The metal edge hurt immediately, but she barely registered it before checking the environmental panel again.

Still no alarm.

If seventeen were releasing a volatile compound, the room sensors either weren't sensitive enough to detect it or weren't looking for the correct chemistry.

Neither option encouraged breathing deeply.

She searched the transfer notes for any mention of aerosolized compounds, altered volatile signaling or secretion through modified stomata. Halfway down the page she realized she hadn't processed the last several lines and went back.

Nothing.

She checked again.

Still nothing.

The irritation behind her nose seemed stronger now.

Was it actually increasing?

Or had noticing it changed how much attention her brain gave it?

She couldn't separate the two anymore.

That was enough.

Isolate the room. Flag the specimen. Go home.

Kellie turned toward the door.

“Seriously?”

She stopped with one foot already moving.

Bram's voice had come from behind her.

Not the hallway.

Not the intercom.

Behind her.

Kellie turned.

The space between the benches was empty.

She listened instead of trusting the first impression. The ventilation fan maintained a steady mechanical hum overhead. A pump vibrated through the irrigation manifold. Somewhere inside the lighting array, a relay switched with a small click.

No footsteps.

“Bram?”

Nothing answered.

She opened the door and checked the nursery beyond it.

Empty.

Her pulse had accelerated enough that each beat pressed against the underside of her jaw.

Kellie checked the environmental display again, partly because chemical exposure was now a legitimate possibility and partly because the numbers gave her somewhere to put her attention.

27.2 Celsius.

61 percent humidity.

Oxygen normal.

Carbon dioxide normal.

Sleep deprivation could cause auditory disturbances. So could dehydration, low blood sugar, migraine symptoms. Chemical exposure had obviously moved higher on the list.

There were explanations.

Several.

She didn't need to know which one yet.

She only needed to leave safely.

Kellie turned back toward the plants.

Specimen seventeen looked different.

The two upper leaves seemed closer together than they had been.

She watched them for several seconds, waiting for airflow to shift them again.

Nothing happened.

Probably the angle.

When she looked toward the far wall, however, the bench remained sharp while the room behind it took an extra moment to resolve. Her vision had been doing that for the last hour.

Exhaustion.

Good enough.

She reached for her tablet to file the isolation report.

Something moved at the edge of her vision.

Kellie's hand stopped.

Seventeen.

The upper leaves curved inward.

Not much. A few millimeters at first, then enough for their shadows to merge.

The dark space between them formed a shape her brain tried to recognize.

A forehead.

Kellie stared.

Two eyes appeared beneath it.

Then a nose, flattened absurdly between green tissue that could not possibly support a human face.

Bram looked back at her.

Too small.

Wrong proportions.

Still unmistakably Bram.

“You're tired already?”

Kellie's next breath stopped before it finished.

The realization came faster than fear.

*I'm hallucinating.*

Not at home after staying awake too long. Not while falling asleep.

At work.

Inside a room containing experimental organisms she had been asked to evaluate.

The implications spread outward before she could stop them. If she couldn't trust what she was seeing, she couldn't trust an inspection based on sight. She couldn't safely operate equipment around another person. She couldn't sign off on data she wasn't sure she had actually observed.

Worst of all, some part of her mind immediately reached for the question she had been postponing for years.

What kind of botanist couldn't trust her own observations?

Kellie blinked.

Bram disappeared.

Only specimen seventeen remained under the grow lamp.

Two pale leaves.

Stem.

Ceramic container.

Nothing else.

She put both hands against the bench and concentrated on the pressure along her palms. The metal edge was hard. Cool. Stable. When she shifted her weight, the pressure changed exactly as it should.

Something she could verify.

Fine.

Kellie opened an incident report.

Her fingers hesitated above the screen before she forced them to type.

**Possible airborne secretion from specimen 17. Unidentified volatile or particulate compound. Recommend immediate isolation pending chemical analysis and personnel evaluation.**

That last part covered her without requiring her to write *I just saw my supervisor growing out of a plant* in a permanent laboratory record.

She started toward the submit button.

“That's what we're going with?”

Kellie's hand stopped.

The voice came from her left.

She didn't turn immediately.

Plants did not speak.

That wasn't a hypothesis requiring further investigation.

Slowly, she raised her head toward specimen nineteen.

Two leaves overlapped beneath the grow lamp.

Bram's eyes opened between them.

He grinned.

Kellie remained where she was, one hand gripping the tablet while every explanation she had assembled a moment ago suddenly seemed much less reassuring.

Farther down the row, something produced a short, broken sound.

Her attention snapped toward it.

The ventilation fan had been running for hours. A damaged bearing could chirp under load. The irrigation pump could cavitate. A valve might have shifted.

Plenty of mechanical explanations.

Then the sound came again.

This time it rose at the end.

A little breath.

A little laugh.

Kellie stared into the row of plants and waited for the machinery to repeat it.

Nothing did.

Then specimen seventeen started laughing too.
